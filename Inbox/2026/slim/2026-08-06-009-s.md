12 Modes of High-Level Thinking (6):

1. Inductive Thinking

6. Conceptual Thinking
7. Scenario Thinking

8. Evidential Thinking

2. Deductive Thinking

Applying general principles to reach
specific conclusions.

Example:

You know untreated infections worsen and predict
that a patient’s symptoms will progress without
treatment.

Takeaway:
Reliable rules allow you to predict specific
outcomes.
[3942594769528427879_3942594583505600385.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

1. Inductive Thinking

Drawing general conclusions from specific
observations.

Example:
You notice that several metals expand when heated
and infer that heat causes metals to expand.

Takeaway:
Patterns across examples can reveal broader
truths.
[3942594769528427879_3942594583513907966.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

3. Systems Thinking

Understanding how components interact
within a larger system.

Example:
You study how public transit, housing costs, and
traffic patterns influence each other in a city.

Takeaway:
Changes in one part of a system can ripple through
the whole.
[3942594769528427879_3942594584059075435.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

5. Algorithmic Thinking

Solving problems through clear step-by-
step procedures.

Example:
You write a sequence of steps that processes data
the same way every time.

Takeaway:
Clear procedures turn complex tasks into
repeatable solutions.
[3942594769528427879_3942594584226928037.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

4. Normative Thinking

Reasoning about how decisions should
be made according to rational or ethical
standards.

Example:

You evaluate a policy by asking whether it’s fair
and ethically justified, not just whether it is
effective.

Takeaway:
Focus on what should be done, not just what is
common.
[3942594769528427879_3942594584386271355.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

6. Conceptual Thinking

Organizing information into broader ideas
and categories.

Example:

You categorize your monthly expenses into
"needs" and "wants" to better understand your
overall spending habits.

Takeaway:
Concepts compress complex information into
usable knowledge.
[3942594769528427879_3942594584830979756.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

7. Scenario Thinking

Exploring multiple possible futures to guide
decisions.

Example:
You imagine how your plans would change if the
economy improves, stays stable, or declines.

Takeaway:
Preparing for several futures leads to more resilient
decisions.
[3942594769528427879_3942594584847722832.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

9. Reductionist Thinking

Breaking complex systems into smaller
components.

Example:

You learn a difficult song on the piano by
practicing one hand at a time before trying to play
them together.

Takeaway:
Complex problems often unravel piece by piece.
[3942594769528427879_3942594584923177219.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

8. Evidential Thinking

Forming beliefs based on the strength and
quality of evidence.

Example:
You revise your opinion after new research
contradicts what you previously believed.

Takeaway:
Strong thinkers update beliefs when evidence
changes.
[3942594769528427879_3942594598563187119.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

10. Mechanistic Thinking

Explaining a phenomenon by understanding
the mechanism that produces it.

Example:

You learn that caffeine works by blocking your
brain's "sleepiness" receptors rather than viewing
coffee as a magic energy boost.

Takeaway:
Understanding how something works explains why
it behaves as it does.
[3942594769528427879_3942594599645281507.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

11. Teleological Thinking

Explaining something by the function or
purpose it serves.

Example:
You understand that bright flower colors evolved to
attract pollinators.

Takeaway:
Understanding function clarifies why certain
features exist.
[3942594769528427879_3942594600534301105.jpg]: The Princeton Review ©
@theprincetonreview

12. Model-Based Thinking

Using simplified representations to
understand or predict outcomes.

Example:
You look at a floor plan of a house to visualize
how your furniture will fit before you move in.

Takeaway:
Good models simplify reality without losing what
matters most.

в рамках более крупной системы.

Вы изучаете, как общественный транспорт, стоимость жилья и 
трафик влияют друг на друга в городе.

Вывод:
Изменения в одной части системы могут распространиться на всю систему.

@theprincetonreview

Решение проблем посредством четких пошаговых процедур.

Обоснование решений в соответствии с рациональными или этическими

Вывод:
Сосредоточьтесь на том, что должно быть сделано, а не только на том, что обычно делается.

Вывод:
Концепции сжимают сложную информацию в полезные знания.
